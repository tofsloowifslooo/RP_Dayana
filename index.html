PLS210 · Research Proposal

What does it mean to be a “good” Muslim?

Imagine a young Kazakh Muslim says “iftar” instead of “ауызашар.”
Someone might say:

“It’s just another word for breaking the fast.”
Someone else might think:

“Why not use the Kazakh word? Isn’t that part of our tradition?”
Is this simply a choice of words — or does it say something about religious and cultural identity?
02 / 16
Context

A small difference can point to a larger question.

In Kazakhstan, questions about Islam can overlap with questions about culture, tradition, and identity.

Different people may attach different meanings to the same religious practice, word, or interpretation.

I am interested in these differences in meaning — rather than assuming one interpretation from the beginning.
03 / 16
Research Question

My research question

How do young Kazakh Muslims account for aspects of Islamic tradition that are publicly contested in Kazakhstan?

I use “account” to mean how participants explain, interpret, negotiate, or make sense of these issues.

Islam as everyday life

Islam can become part of personal ethics and everyday life.

Omelicheva discusses the individualization and intimization of Islam in Kazakhstan, showing how religious commitment can become connected to personal ways of living.

This helps me see religious identity not only as institutional membership, but also as something interpreted personally.

Literature Review

Who defines “Traditional Islam”?

“Traditional Islam” is not simply a neutral description.

Recent scholarship examines how the category is connected to Hanafi jurisprudence, Maturidi theology, local customs, religious institutions, and state regulation.

This raises a question about religious authority: whose interpretation becomes legitimate?

Kerim & Kurmanaliyev (2026)

Literature Review

Islam and Kazakh identity

Religious identity can also intersect with competing ideas of Kazakhness.

Scholarship examines tensions between Islamic practices and ethnonational ideas of Kazakh identity, including debates over what is considered local, foreign, traditional, or reformist.

But these categories may look different from the perspective of young Muslims themselves.

Sikhimbayeva & Sadvokassova (2025)

Interpretivist Tradition

Analytical Framework

Boundary-making

12 / 16
Positionality

I am not an external observer.

I am Kazakh, and my family is Muslim.

This gives me some familiarity with the cultural and religious context I am studying.

At the same time, this familiarity can shape what I notice, what I take for granted, and how I interpret participants' accounts.

My goal is therefore not to pretend to be completely detached, but to remain reflexive about my own position.
