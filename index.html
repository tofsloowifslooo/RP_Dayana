Research Proposal · PLS210

What does it mean to be a “good” Muslim?

Imagine a young Kazakh Muslim says “iftar” instead of “ауызашар.”
Someone might say:

“It’s just another word for breaking the fast.”
Someone else might think:

“Why not use the Kazakh word? Isn’t that part of our tradition?”
Is this simply a choice of words — or does it say something about religious and cultural identity?
02 / 15
From a small example to a larger issue

This tension is not only hypothetical.

President Kassym-Jomart Tokayev has publicly commented on questions surrounding religious practice, Kazakh culture, and what should be understood as appropriate religious expression.

One example discussed in public debate concerns the relationship between visible Islamic practices and ideas of Kazakh cultural tradition.

The point is not whether one interpretation is “correct” before the research begins.

The point is that different meanings can exist around the same practice.

So the question becomes: how do young Muslims themselves navigate these meanings?

03 / 15
The puzzle

What is actually being contested?

Religion

What counts as an Islamic practice? How should it be interpreted?

Tradition

Which practices are understood as part of Kazakh Muslim tradition?

Identity

How can someone understand themselves as both Muslim and Kazakh?

And importantly: who gets to define these boundaries?

04 / 15
Research Question

This leads to my question.

How do young Kazakh Muslims account for aspects of Islamic tradition that are publicly contested in Kazakhstan?

I am interested not simply in identifying what is contested, but in understanding how participants explain and interpret it.

05 / 15
Key Concept

What does “account” mean?

Explain

Explain how they understand a practice or disagreement.

Interpret

Give meaning to religious experiences and traditions.

Negotiate

Navigate between different interpretations without necessarily accepting one completely.

I do not assume that participants need to defend or justify their beliefs.

Literature · Starting Point

Islam as a “way of life”

Islam is not necessarily experienced only through formal religious institutions.

Omelicheva discusses processes of individualization and intimization of Islam in Kazakhstan.

Religious ideas can become connected to personal ethics, everyday choices, and understandings of how to live.

If Islam becomes part of everyday life, people also have to interpret what it means in their own circumstances.

Omelicheva (2011)

Research Gap

From structures → to meanings

Existing literature My study
How is Islam regulated? How do young Muslims understand Islam?
How is “Traditional Islam” constructed? How do young Muslims interpret religious authority?
How does piety intersect with identity? How do people make sense of that intersection?

My gap is therefore an analytical shift toward participants' meaning-making.

Analytical Framework

Three sensitizing concepts

01

Meaning-making

How do participants understand religious practices and disagreements?

02

Religious authority

Whose interpretation is treated as credible? Family, imam, scripture, online teacher, or personal experience?

03

Boundary-making

How are boundaries such as Islamic / un-Islamic, Kazakh / foreign, and traditional / non-traditional constructed?

These concepts sensitize me to possible meanings. They do not determine what participants must say.
