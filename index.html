01 — Hook

What does it mean to be a “good” Muslim?

Imagine a young Kazakh Muslim says “iftar” instead of “ауызашар.”

Someone might say:

“It’s just another word for breaking the fast.”

Someone else might think:

“Why not use the Kazakh word? Isn’t that part of our tradition?”

Is this simply a choice of words — or does it say something about religious and cultural identity?

02 — Context

A small difference can point to a larger question.

In Kazakhstan, questions about Islam can overlap with questions about culture, tradition, and identity.

In 2024, President Kassym-Jomart Tokayev spoke publicly about women wearing all-black clothing, connecting the issue to perceptions of Kazakh tradition, religious influence, and national identity.

Tengrinews reported that Tokayev argued that dressing entirely in black was contrary to the worldview of the Kazakh people and warned against distancing from spiritual roots and national identity.

Source: Tengrinews.kz, 22 May 2024.

03 — Research Question

My research question

How do young Kazakh Muslims account for aspects of Islamic tradition that are publicly contested in Kazakhstan?

Here, “account” means how participants explain, interpret, negotiate, or make sense of something that is contested.

04 — Epistemological Position

Interpretivism

I am interested not only in what people do, but in what those practices mean to them.

Meaning

People interpret their social and religious worlds through meanings they have developed.

Context

Meanings are shaped by family, community, history, religion, and public discourse.

Understanding

My aim is to understand participants’ interpretations rather than impose one definition beforehand.

05 — Literature Review

Islam as everyday life

Omelicheva (2011)

Omelicheva describes contemporary Islam in Kazakhstan as increasingly individualized and connected to everyday life, including personal ethics and ways of living.

This suggests that Islam cannot be understood only through formal institutions or official religious organizations.
06 — Literature Review

Islam in public life

Malik (2019)

Malik examines the public revival of Islam in Kazakhstan through processes of institutionalisation.

Religious revival can become visible through mosque attendance, halal markets, moral dress, youth participation, and other social practices.

Religion is therefore not simply a private matter.
07 — Literature Review

What counts as “Traditional Islam”?

Kerim & Kurmanaliyev (2026)

The authors examine how “Traditional Islam” in Kazakhstan has developed through history, state discourse, religious authority, and contemporary debates.

The category connects Hanafi jurisprudence, Maturidi theology, local customs, and institutional religious authority.

This raises a question: who gets to define what counts as authentic or traditional?
08 — Literature Review

Islam and Kazakh identity

Sikhimbayeva & Sadvokassova (2025)

Their research examines the relationship between Islam and ethnonational identity in post-Soviet Kazakhstan.

They discuss tensions between certain forms of Islamic practice and ideas of Kazakhness, including debates around local customs and visible religious practices.

Religious practice can therefore become connected to questions of cultural belonging.
09 — Literature Synthesis

What does the literature tell us?

Everyday Islam

Islam can be experienced through everyday values, practices, and personal choices.

Public Religion

Religious revival also has visible social and institutional dimensions.

Identity & Authority

Questions of religious authenticity can overlap with national identity and institutional authority.

But these perspectives leave room for a more micro-level question: how do young Muslims themselves make sense of these tensions?

10 — Research Gap

What is missing?

Existing research provides substantial analysis of state regulation, securitisation, religious authority, national identity, and youth religiosity.

What is less developed is the micro-level question of how young Kazakh Muslims themselves interpret and explain contested aspects of religious tradition.

My study therefore shifts the focus from how contestation is structured to how it is experienced and interpreted.

11 — Analytical Framework

How will I interpret participants’ accounts?

Meaning-making

How participants explain what religious practices mean to them.

Religious authority

Whose interpretations participants recognise as legitimate or convincing.

Boundary-making

How participants distinguish between practices, identities, and understandings of Islam.

These are sensitizing concepts, not categories that I will impose on participants in advance.

12 — Positionality

I am not an external observer.

I am Kazakh, and I was raised in a Muslim family. For me, Islam is familiar primarily as a family tradition and cultural background.

This gives me familiarity with the cultural and religious context I am studying, but it also means I bring assumptions and experiences into the research.

Rather than pretending to be completely detached, I need to remain reflexive about how my position shapes what I notice and how I interpret participants’ accounts.
13 — Method

How would I study this?

A qualitative, interpretivist approach using semi-structured interviews with young Kazakh Muslims.

Recruit participants
→
Semi-structured interviews
→
Participant accounts
→
Interpretive analysis

The interviews would allow participants to identify what they themselves consider contested, rather than beginning with a fixed definition of religious contestation.

14 — Contribution

What can this study contribute?

Empirical

Brings young Muslims’ own accounts into the study of religious contestation in Kazakhstan.

Analytical

Connects everyday meaning-making with questions of religious authority and identity.

Reflexive

Makes the researcher’s cultural position part of the methodological discussion.
