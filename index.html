When does a religious practice become a question of identity?

Imagine a young Kazakh Muslim says “iftar” instead of “ауызашар.”

Someone might say:

“It’s just another word for breaking the fast.”

Someone else might think:

“Why not use the Kazakh word? Isn’t that part of our tradition?”

Is this simply a choice of words — or does it say something about religious and cultural identity?

CONTEXT

Islam in Kazakhstan is not experienced in only one way.

Religious revival

Islam became more publicly visible after independence through institutions, religious practice, and new forms of participation.

Traditional Islam

The idea of “traditional Islam” is connected to local history, Hanafi jurisprudence, Maturidi theology, and institutional authority.

National identity

Religious practices can also be interpreted through ideas of Kazakhness, culture, language, and national tradition.

RESEARCH QUESTION

How do young Kazakh Muslims account for aspects of Islamic tradition that are publicly contested in Kazakhstan?

Here, “account” means how people explain, interpret, negotiate, or reinterpret something in their own words.

EPISTEMOLOGICAL POSITIONING

Interpretivism

I approach this question from an interpretivist perspective.

Interpretivism focuses on how people create and understand meaning in their social worlds.

Rather than asking whether one interpretation of Islamic tradition is objectively correct, I ask how young Muslims themselves understand and explain contested practices.

The aim is therefore to understand participants’ meanings rather than impose a single definition of Islam or tradition.

LITERATURE REVIEW

Islam as everyday and public life

Omelicheva (2011)

“Placing emphasis on personal ethics and personal fulfilment as well as having private beliefs in God and searching for true meaning of life follows naturally from a conception of Muslimness in Kazakhstan as a way of life, rather than strict adherence to Islamic laws and prohibitions.”

Individualization and intimization: a growing contemporary trend toward the individualization and intimization of Islam, where believers seek personalized ways of practicing faith, personal ethics, spiritual fulfilment, and private, intimate experiences of God.

Malik (2019)

  • A 37-fold increase in mosques (from 68 in 1991 to 2,516 in 2016).
  • Attendance at daily and Friday congregational prayers (jum’ah) is predominantly driven by young and middle-aged people.
  • Halal market: rapid expansion of halal slaughterhouses (over 130) and halal businesses (over 600), creating what Marlene Laruelle terms a “bourgeois Islam” practiced and exhibited by the urban middle class.

LITERATURE SYNTHESIS

What does the literature already tell us?

Omelicheva

Islam as personal and everyday meaning.

Evidence: Islam operates as an intimate, everyday “way of life” and personal ethical orientation, expressed through local rituals, household life-cycle ceremonies, and individual spiritual experience rather than strict canonical jurisprudence.

Malik

Islam as visible public and social life.

Evidence: Re-Islamization produced a “public revitalisation” and “social sacralisation” of Islam, moving faith from a private matter into visible public, social, economic (halal markets), and digital spaces.

Kerim & Kurmanaliyev

Traditional Islam as historically and institutionally constructed.

Evidence: “Traditional Islam” (Hanafi-Maturidi fused with local custom) is presented as a state-mediated construct and an instrument of symbolic power, used by state-aligned institutions such as SAMK to regulate the religious sphere and define orthodoxy.

Sikhimbayeva & Sadvokassova

Islamic practice and Kazakh national identity can intersect.

Evidence: Islamic practice and Kazakh national identity are not inherently opposed: religion and ethnonationalism intersect and can form a compatible synthesis within the national framework.

Bektenova et al.

Youth religiosity and tensions around contemporary religious identity and practice.

Evidence: Young people (ages 18–24) are the largest group among practicing Muslims, yet experience “identity fragmentation” and public friction over visible piety, such as hijab in secular schools and public prayer spaces.

Zakhaya et al.

Tensions between “traditional” and newer or non-traditional forms of religiosity among young Kazakhs.

Evidence: State-supported “traditional” Kazakh Islam clashes with newer orientations (such as Salafism or new religiosities) adopted by young Kazakhs seeking moral certainty, creating internal rifts and public anxiety.

Kalybekova et al.

Official theological responses to customs such as betashar that reformist interpretations contest.

Evidence: Official theological arguments defend customary rites such as betashar (the bridal greeting ritual) as vital national values, rebutting reformist claims that they constitute shirk.

RESEARCH GAP

What is less developed?

Existing research: macro level

Institutions, regulation, religious authority, national identity, securitisation, and broader patterns of youth religiosity.

My focus: micro level

How young Kazakh Muslims themselves interpret and explain contested aspects of religious tradition in everyday social contexts.

In the literature I reviewed, the micro-level question is less developed: how young Muslims make sense of contested tradition in their own words. My study shifts the focus from how contestation is structured publicly to how it is experienced and interpreted by young Muslims themselves.
ANALYTICAL FRAMEWORK

How will I interpret participants’ accounts?

I use three sensitising concepts rather than fixed categories.

Meaning-making

How participants explain what a religious practice means to them.

Religious authority

How participants refer to scholars, institutions, family, texts, community, or personal experience when explaining tradition.

Boundary-making

How participants distinguish between practices they see as acceptable, traditional, religious, cultural, or contested.

These concepts guide what I notice, but participants remain free to describe contestation in their own terms.

POSITIONALITY

I am not a completely objective outsider.

I am Kazakh, and I was raised in a Muslim family.

For me, Islam is familiar primarily as a family tradition and cultural background. This means I already have some familiarity with the cultural and religious context I am studying.

At the same time, this background can shape what I notice, what I take for granted, and how I interpret participants’ accounts.

I therefore cannot approach the research as a completely objective outsider. I need to remain reflexive about how my own position shapes the research process and interpretation.
METHOD

From interpretation to interviews

Interpretivism
→
Qualitative approach
→
Semi-structured interviews
→
Participant accounts
→
Interpretive analysis

Participants

I would purposively recruit young Kazakh Muslims who can speak about their own experiences and understandings of religious tradition.

Interviews

Semi-structured, open-ended questions would give participants space to explain their own meanings rather than choose from predetermined answers.

Analysis

I would interpret recurring meanings, explanations, and distinctions in participants’ accounts while remaining reflexive about my own position.

I will allow participants to identify what they themselves experience as contested, rather than imposing a fixed definition of contestation in advance.

CONTRIBUTION

What can this study add?

The study can contribute a micro-level perspective to research that has often focused on institutions, public discourse, religious authority, and national identity.

By focusing on young Muslims’ own accounts, I can examine how people navigate competing ideas of religion, tradition, culture, and identity in their everyday lives.

The goal is not to decide which interpretation of tradition is correct, but to understand how people make sense of disagreement around it.
