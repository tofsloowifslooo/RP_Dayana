Research Proposal · PLS210

What does it mean to be a “good” Muslim?

Imagine someone tells you:

“This is a religious practice, but it is not really Kazakh.”

Take a few seconds. Don't decide whether the statement is right or wrong. Instead, ask:

Who gets to decide what counts as both Islamic and Kazakh?

01 · Research Question

The question

How do young Kazakh Muslims account for aspects of Islamic tradition that are publicly contested in Kazakhstan?

Young Kazakh Muslims

The people whose interpretations and experiences are at the centre of the study.

“Account for”

To explain, interpret, negotiate, justify, reinterpret, or otherwise make something intelligible.

“Publicly contested”

Religious practices or interpretations that are questioned, disputed, or debated in social contexts.

02 · Starting Point

Islam as a “way of life”

Islamic revival is not necessarily only about joining religious institutions.

Omelicheva's analysis points toward the individualization and intimization of Islam: religious ideas can become part of personal ethics, everyday choices, and understandings of how to live.

If Islam can become part of everyday moral life, then religious meaning is also something people actively interpret.

Omelicheva, M. Y. (2011). Islam in Kazakhstan: A survey of contemporary trends and sources of securitization.

03 · Public Religion

Individualized ≠ private

Personal religious practice can become socially visible.

Prayer

Religious practice can enter everyday social spaces.

Dress

Religious commitments can become visible through appearance.

Consumption

Halal markets and consumption can make religious identity socially visible.

Malik's account of the public revival of Islam suggests that religion is not simply a private matter.

Malik (2019). Islam in Post-Soviet Kazakhstan: Experiencing Public Revival of Islam through Institutionalisation.

04 · Religious Authority

Who defines legitimate Islam?

What exactly is meant by “Traditional Islam”?

Kerim and Kurmanaliyev examine “Traditional Islam” as a state-mediated and institutionally shaped category involving:

Hanafi jurisprudence

A particular Islamic legal tradition.

Maturidi theology

A theological tradition associated with the official framing.

Local customs

Historical and cultural practices connected to Kazakh religious life.

This raises a question about religious authority: whose interpretation counts as legitimate?

Kerim & Kurmanaliyev (2026). Traditional Islam in Kazakhstan.

05 · Identity

When religious identity meets national identity

What does it mean to be Muslim?
What does it mean to be Kazakh?

Sikhimbayeva and Sadvokassova examine the relationship between Islam and ethnonational identity in post-Soviet Kazakhstan.

Their analysis shows how religious practices and ideas about Kazakh identity can sometimes reinforce one another, while at other times being interpreted as incompatible.

The important point is not to assume the answer, but to ask how people themselves understand the relationship.

Sikhimbayeva & Sadvokassova (2025). Re-defining Kazakh national identity: interplay of Islam and ethnonationalism in post-Soviet nation-building in Kazakhstan.

06 · The Terrain

Competing interpretations

The literature suggests several possible sites where interpretations of Islamic tradition may come into tension.

Religious practice

How should a particular practice be understood?

Tradition

Which customs are religiously meaningful, cultural, or both?

Authority

Whose interpretation should be trusted?

But these should not be treated as fixed camps. Participants may combine, negotiate, or reject different interpretations.

07 · Literature Review

What do we already know?

Literature tells us What it helps us see
Islam can become individualized. Religious meaning may be connected to everyday personal life.
Islamic revival has a public dimension. Personal religious choices can become socially visible.
“Traditional Islam” is institutionally shaped. Questions of religious authority matter.
Islam intersects with Kazakh identity. Religious and national boundaries can become connected.
Youth participate in changing forms of religiosity. Young people are important actors in these processes.

The literature gives us the terrain.

08 · Research Gap

From structures → to meanings

Existing literature asks My study asks
How is Islam regulated? How do young Muslims understand Islam?
How is “Traditional Islam” constructed? How do young Muslims interpret religious authority?
How does piety intersect with identity? How do people make sense of that intersection?
What patterns exist among young Muslims? How do individuals explain their own experiences?
The literature I reviewed provides substantial analysis of regulation, institutional authority, national identity, and broader patterns of religiosity. My study shifts the analytical focus toward how young Kazakh Muslims themselves make sense of contested tradition.
09 · Key Concept

Why “account”?

I use “account” broadly to mean the ways participants explain and make their religious positions intelligible.

Explain

“This is how I understand this practice.”

Negotiate

“I understand both sides, but I do something different.”

Reinterpret

“I understand the tradition differently from how it is usually presented.”

I do not assume in advance which of these forms of meaning-making participants will use.

10 · Analytical Framework

Three sensitizing concepts

These concepts guide what I pay attention to without determining what participants must say.

01

Meaning-making

How do participants understand religious practices, experiences, and disagreements?

02

Religious authority

Whose interpretation is treated as credible? Family? Imam? Scripture? Online teacher? Personal experience?

03

Boundary-making

How are boundaries between Islamic / un-Islamic, Kazakh / foreign, traditional / non-traditional constructed or negotiated?

The framework sensitizes me to possible meanings; it does not determine in advance what those meanings must be.
11 · Positionality

Who defines the problem?

As a researcher, I enter the project already familiar with concepts such as “Traditional Islam,” religious revival, securitisation, and identity contestation.

The risk is treating my analytical categories as if they were automatically participants' categories.

Instead of asking:

“How do you deal with discrimination against your Islamic practice?”

I can ask:

“Can you tell me about a situation where your understanding of Islam differed from someone else's?”

This leaves room for participants to identify what matters to them.

13 · Contribution

What could this study add?

Empirical contribution

A closer account of how young Kazakh Muslims interpret and explain contested aspects of religious tradition.

Conceptual contribution

Connecting broader processes such as individualization, public religion, and institutional authority with micro-level meaning-making.

Scope

The aim is not to represent all young Kazakh Muslims, but to illuminate how particular participants understand their experiences.

15 · Reflection

What I still don't understand

AI helped me understand the difference between studying a phenomenon and studying how people make sense of that phenomenon.

But several questions remain:

01

How can I recognize participant meanings without imposing my own?

02

How much should “publicly contested” be defined before the interviews?

03

How can I distinguish participants' interpretations from what they think I expect them to say?

The literature tells me what the terrain looks like.

My research asks how people experience and interpret that terrain.
